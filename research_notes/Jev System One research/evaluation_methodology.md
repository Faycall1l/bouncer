# Evaluation Methodology for Fast‑Weight / System‑One Models in Real‑Time Content Moderation

> Scope note. As of Nov 2026 there is little peer‑reviewed literature that evaluates *weight‑updating* fast‑weight / test‑time‑training (TTT) models for moderation. The closest directly relevant 2026 evidence is a single, non‑peer‑reviewed paired evaluation of two "System‑1" decision models (Laya, Jev) on agent‑harness decisions ([Li, arXiv 2610.02267, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1)), plus the Laya project's own README/benchmark claims ([NandhaKishorM/laya, 2026](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya)). Established practice is drawn from ACL/USENIX/NIST‑adjacent evaluation literature; newer System‑1‑specific findings are labelled as such. Vendor claims are labelled as vendor claims.

## Key Question 1: What datasets and protocols are standard for toxic-content and rule-based moderation, and which are appropriate for dynamic per-community rule sets?

### Takeaway
For toxic content, the standard stack is Jigsaw/Civil Comments (with identity and subgroup annotations), HateCheck‑style functional tests, and domain‑matched data such as ToxicChat; for *dynamic per‑community rules*, the only well‑matched public resource is NORMVIO (community‑rule violations across 3.2K subreddits), and it should be combined with held‑out‑community/held‑out‑rule splits plus probe datasets like HateCheck, DynaHate, and HateBench rather than a single static toxicity test set.

### Cited Findings
- The Jigsaw Unintended Bias in Toxicity Classification dataset (Civil Comments) is a >1M‑comment English corpus with a continuous `target` toxicity score, six toxicity subtypes (severe_toxicity, obscene, identity_attack, insult, threat, sexual_explicit), per‑identity mention labels for ~18 identity categories, and expanded train/public/private test splits with subgroup labels — i.e., it is explicitly designed for subgroup false‑positive analysis, not just aggregate accuracy — [Hugging Face dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/jigsaw_unintended_bias); [Kaggle competition data](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/jigsaw-unintended-bias-in-toxicity-classification/data).
- Identity‑term bias is a documented property of toxicity classifiers: comments mentioning identity terms have higher observed toxicity incidence, so models learn spurious identity→toxicity associations; this motivated the Jigsaw bias metric and follow‑up work — [Resolving Sources of Bias in Toxicity Classifiers, arXiv 2006.16402](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.16402v1); [Jigsaw Kaggle data description](https://www.kaggle.com/c/jigsaw-unintended-bias-in-toxicity-classification/data).
- HateCheck (ACL‑IJCNLP 2021) argues that held‑out test F1/accuracy "makes it difficult to identify specific model weak points" and "risks overestimating generalisable model performance"; it defines 29 model functionalities from a literature review and civil‑society interviews, with validated test cases — [Röttger et al., ACL 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.4/). The released suite has 3,728 validated cases; 18 functionalities cover hateful content and 11 cover challenging non‑hateful content — [HateCheck data](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Paul/hatecheck); [Alan Turing Institute summary](https://www.turing.ac.uk/blog/finding-critical-weaknesses-ai-models-hate-speech-detection-hatecheck).
- DynaHate (ACL 2021) uses a human‑and‑model‑in‑the‑loop process to generate datasets dynamically; the paper explicitly argues for dynamic benchmarking and contrast sets that are developed in a directed, adaptive way — [Vidgen et al., ACL 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.132.pdf); [arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/html/2012.15761v2).
- HateBench (USENIX Security 2025) contributes 7,838 manually annotated LLM‑generated samples (3,641 hate / 4,197 non‑hate) across 34 identity groups and six LLMs, benchmarks eight detectors (Perspective, OpenAI Moderation, Detoxify original/unbiased, LFTW, TweetHate, BERT‑HateXplain), and reports that detector performance degrades with newer LLMs; adversarial hate campaigns reach attack success rate >0.966 against Perspective, Moderation and TweetHate, and model‑stealing attacks improve attack efficiency 13–21× — [HateBench project page](https://hatebench.xinyueshen.me/); [USENIX paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/shen).
- ToxicChat (LMSYS, 2023) is the first toxicity benchmark from real user–chatbot interactions: 10,166 first‑turn queries, 7.18% toxic and 1.78% jailbreak, with strict majority labels (≥3 of 4 annotators; 96.11% trial agreement). Perspective API pre‑filtering removed ~60% of annotation work at an estimated cost of 1 missed toxic example in 48 on the trial set — [LMSYS ToxicChat blog](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-10-30-toxicchat/); [Findings‑EMNLP paper](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.311.pdf).
- Domain shift is large enough to invalidate "standard" leaderboard transfer: on ToxicChat, OpenAI Moderation scored 84.3 precision / 11.7 recall / 20.6 F1 and Perspective 90.9 / 2.7 / 5.3, while a RoBERTa trained on ToxicChat scored 75.9 / 68.7 / 72.1 versus 28.4 F1 when trained on Jigsaw data — [LMSYS ToxicChat results](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-10-30-toxicchat/).
- For community‑specific rules, NORMVIO (Findings of EMNLP 2021) contains 51K conversations across 3.2K subreddits with community‑specific judgements of *which rule* was violated, and maps 25K community rules into nine categories — [Park et al., ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2021.findings-emnlp.288.pdf); [arXiv 2110.04419](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.04419).
- On Reddit, rules and norms are only loosely coupled; many norms are implicit and moderators enforce them without a written rule — [Chandrasekharan et al., 2018](https://shagunjhaver.com/research/articles/chandrasekharan-2018-norms/chandrasekharan-2018-norms.pdf). A 2025 study extracts hidden moderation criteria from historical moderation decisions using an interpretable approach, and warns that patterns predicting decisions may not represent genuine community norms — [Kim et al., arXiv 2509.02926](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02926); [HTML version](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02926v1).
- Platform‑scale protocol: Meta's Community Standards Enforcement Report uses sampled content *views* labelled by human reviewers (often 2 reviewers plus a third tiebreaker), stratified sampling, 95% confidence windows, and 95% upper limits when violations are too rare to estimate; changes within those windows are not treated as real changes — [Meta prevalence metric](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/improving/prevalence-metric/). Proactive rate = share of actioned content found before users reported it, with explicit caveats that it excludes undetected violations and says nothing about time‑to‑detection or views before detection — [Meta proactive rate](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/improving/proactive-rate-metric/). An independent advisory group found these three metrics (prevalence, actioned content, proactivity) reasonable but recommended reporting prevalence two ways, relating it to real‑world harm, and reporting subpopulations — [DTAG report, 2019](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/area/center/justice/document/dtag_report_5.22.2019.pdf); [Meta summary](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/05/dtag-report/).
- A 2026 unified benchmark for LLM moderators spans 49 categories and evaluates moderators across in‑domain and out‑of‑domain datasets to quantify generalization and failure modes — [arXiv 2508.07063](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07063v1). ToxiGen is reported as ~274K machine‑generated toxic/benign statements covering 13 minority groups, including implicit hate — [Evidently AI benchmark overview](https://www.evidentlyai.com/blog/llm-safety-bias-benchmarks) (secondary source).

### Inferences
- **Established practice for a static moderation benchmark**: combine (a) a source‑matched corpus (Jigsaw/Civil Comments for public comments; ToxicChat for chatbot traffic), (b) identity/subgroup slices from Jigsaw, (c) a functional test suite (HateCheck) and (d) an adversarial/paraphrase suite (DynaHate, HateBench, ToxiGen). Suggested implementation: keep HateCheck and HateBench as *frozen probes* never used for threshold fitting.
- **For dynamic per‑community rules specifically**, no single off‑the‑shelf dataset covers arbitrary user‑written rules. The defensible local protocol is: (1) NORMVIO as the public community‑rule benchmark; (2) a private split by *community* and by *rule*, so a rule set seen in fit/calibration never appears in evaluation (analogous to the 2026 System‑1 paper's newly composed B2/G2/J2 controls); (3) per‑rule labels from a written policy, with disagreements adjudicated and agreement reported; (4) a "no‑rule / benign" slice drawn from the same community to measure over‑enforcement. This is an inference from the NORMVIO design, the community‑norms papers, and the held‑out‑rule/threshold lessons in [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- **Label policy must be written down.** In the 2026 System‑1 study, a written safety policy applied by a different LLM agreed with dataset labels on only 72.8% of 540 items; this is a direct warning that "gold" labels for community rules are policy‑dependent — [Li 2026, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Modest‑compute feasibility: all of the above are text‑only datasets; Jigsaw, HateCheck and ToxicChat are available as Hugging Face datasets and can be evaluated with a small classifier or local System‑1 model without training a large model — [HF Jigsaw card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/jigsaw_unintended_bias); [HF HateCheck](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Paul/hatecheck); [ToxicChat](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-10-30-toxicchat/).

### Gaps
- No dataset found that pairs *user‑written, mutable community rules* with newly collected, post‑rule‑change violations and repeated temporal snapshots; "held‑out rule sets" therefore must be constructed locally.
- No reliable public evaluation found for Jev specifically on toxicity/community‑rule moderation; the 2026 evaluation covers agent‑harness decisions, not moderation — [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- No direct evidence found on evaluating *weight‑updating* fast‑weight/TTT models under rule drift; Laya's continuous‑learning claims are vendor README claims — [Laya README](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya).

## Key Question 2: Which metrics matter beyond accuracy (automation precision/recall, calibration/ECE, abstention/escalation rate, latency percentiles, cost, robustness)?

### Takeaway
A moderation eval should report a *policy curve* rather than a single score: precision/recall (or FPR/FNR) at a fixed automation coverage, abstention/escalation rate, call rate and held‑out miss rate for gates, calibration (ECE plus reliability diagram) per rule/community, p50/p95/p99 latency with sample counts, fully loaded cost, and stratified robustness numbers — all with base rates stated.

### Cited Findings
- Selective prediction is defined by the coverage–risk tradeoff: coverage = answered/all, selective risk = errors among answered; risk–coverage curves, AURC and Cov@risk are standard summaries — [Geifman & El‑Yaniv, NeurIPS 2017, as cited in Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1); [selective prediction explainer with worked coverage/risk example](https://arize.com/glossary/abstention/).
- For a *gate*, the 2026 System‑1 paper recommends reporting the share of traffic sent to the expensive path (**call rate**) and the share of cases needing that path that skip it (**miss rate / FNR**), not gate classification accuracy. In their RAG gate, a 5% miss budget kept 92.4% (Laya) / 79.8% (Jev) of LLM calls; "45–58%" in the first draft was gate accuracy mislabelled as end‑to‑end correctness (true end‑to‑end: 97.9% of answerable queries still answered) — [Li 2026, §6–7](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Savings are bounded by base rate: in that synthetic mix a perfect gate could save at most 58%; on real top‑5 RAG contexts both systems recognised under 40% of "no answer here" cases, so a skip gate was judged unsafe — [Li 2026, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Fully loaded cost matters: a pre‑screen at τ=0.1 appeared to save 23.9% of safety‑review cost but saved 4.3% once the pre‑screen's own tokens were counted; at τ=0.05 the two‑tier system cost 4.7% *more*. Jev used ~759 tokens/decision (≈$32 per million decisions at $0.042/M tokens); Laya's marginal cost is local compute — [Li 2026, §5–7](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Calibration numbers from that evaluation: pooled ECE 0.158 (Laya) vs 0.143 (Jev) on one suite, 0.198 vs 0.080 on another; 26% of Laya's answers with confidence ≥0.9 were wrong; per‑domain Platt scaling fitted on half the data reduced Laya's ECE on the other half from 0.22 to 0.027 (Jev 0.21→0.10) — [Li 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Threshold quality is sample‑size dependent: choosing a threshold on one half for a 5% FNR target and evaluating on the other half gave median FNR near 5%, but 95th percentile 6–8% for a track with 800 positives and 10–17% for tracks with 100–200 positives, exceeding target in about half of splits — [Li 2026, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- **Error direction matters more than accuracy for guards.** On a tool‑call guard Laya said "call a tool" for 74.7% of requests needing none (Jev 40.0%); on pre‑execution safety Laya passed 81.5% of harmful requests (Jev 25.5%); on PII egress Laya's AUROC was 0.41 (ranking classes backwards) — [Li 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Robustness is multi‑axis and should be reported separately: reversing option order changed 30.3% of Laya's decisions vs 1.8% of Jev's; rewording instructions plus resampling moved accuracy by 5–15 pp *per track for both systems*, giving each headline number a ~±5 pp wording band; with 50 nearest‑neighbour distractors Laya scored 31.3% (Jev 98.9% on items with a unique valid tool) — [Li 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Detection robustness decays with embedding style: with a payload embedded in a document, Laya detected only 47.5–50.8% of attacks versus 70.8–82.5% for standalone payloads; Jev detected 88.3–94.2% standalone and 90.8–93.3% embedded — [Li 2026, §7](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Adversarial robustness at scale: HateBench reports >0.966 attack success rate for the strongest adversarial attack against several detectors and 13–21× efficiency gains via model stealing — [HateBench](https://hatebench.xinyueshen.me/).
- Platform‑level outcome metrics complement classifier metrics: view‑weighted prevalence with 95% intervals and upper limits for rare categories; proactive rate with the caveat that it ignores undetected violations and time‑to‑detection — [Meta prevalence](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/improving/prevalence-metric/); [Meta proactive rate](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/improving/proactive-rate-metric/).
- Latency practice: report p50 plus p95/p99 (not just the mean), state sample counts (thousands of samples needed for a stable p99), do not average per‑instance percentiles, do not sum stage percentiles, and beware "coordinated omission" in closed‑loop load generators (use fixed‑rate tools such as wrk2) — [Redis p95 guide](https://redis.io/blog/p95-latency/); [Cartesia p95/p99 guide, Oct 2026](https://www.cartesia.ai/learn/p95-p99-latency); [Aerospike p99 guide](https://aerospike.com/blog/what-is-p99-latency/).
- Latency is deployment‑specific: Laya ran 31 ms p50 on an RTX 2080 Ti but 200 ms on an Apple M4 CPU; Jev was 325 ms p50 from one client site and 83 ms from another, so an earlier "10× faster" claim was a property of one network path — [Li 2026, §5 and §7](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).

### Inferences
- **Recommended minimum metric set (inference, grounded in the cited sources):** (1) per‑rule precision/recall and FPR/FNR with Wilson intervals, plus macro‑averages across rules; (2) precision and FPR at fixed automation coverage (e.g., 50/80/95%) and the coverage achieved at a fixed miss budget; (3) abstention/escalation rate and human review load; (4) per‑community and per‑identity‑term FPR slices; (5) ECE and a reliability diagram per rule family, *after* out‑of‑sample calibration; (6) p50/p95/p99 end‑to‑end latency with N, hardware and client region; (7) cost per 1K/1M items including every component and LLM fallback tokens; (8) robustness deltas on HateCheck/DynaHate/HateBench‑style paraphrases and embedded payloads.
- Because moderation errors are asymmetric, optimising F1 hides the relevant risk: report **false‑negative rate on violating content** and **false‑positive rate on benign content** separately, with the operating threshold fixed before evaluation.
- A "fast" model's value in a moderation cascade may be latency rather than money: in the 2026 study, 34–41% of requests were released in ~0.3 s instead of waiting for a multi‑second LLM review, while cost savings were small or negative — [Li 2026, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1). Suggested analogue: measure time‑to‑action for auto‑approved content, not only model call latency.
- Vendor‑reported calibration/escalation features (Laya 0.5's conformal `prediction_set`, `abstain`, `unbound`, `route`) are directly implementable locally, but the vendor itself reports that `unbound` fired on 71% of normal Banking77 rows and recommends gating on `route` rather than a confidence number — [Laya README](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya). Treat these as vendor claims to verify on your own rule sets.

### Gaps
- No peer‑reviewed study found that defines an accepted end‑to‑end metric bundle specifically for *dynamic community‑rule* moderation; the recommended bundle above is a synthesis.
- No evidence found on the human cost/quality of human escalation queues (reviewer SLA, fatigue) in this literature; review load is reported but not human outcomes.
- No standard "cost per avoided harm" or harm‑weighted metric found; Meta's DTAG explicitly calls for relating prevalence to real‑world harm but does not define a formula — [DTAG report](https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/area/center/justice/document/dtag_report_5.22.2019.pdf).

## Key Question 3: How should baselines be chosen (regex, classical classifier, fine-tuned BERT, LLM judge) and what would constitute a fair comparison?

### Takeaway
Use a ladder of baselines — trivial majority/prior, regex/keyword or NER+pattern rules, TF‑IDF/logistic regression, fine‑tuned encoder (BERT/RoBERTa), commercial moderation API, zero‑shot LLM judge, and the System‑1 model — and make the comparison paired: byte‑identical inputs, identical rule text and option order, thresholds fitted on a separate split, and all component costs counted.

### Cited Findings
- The ToxicChat baseline suite is a concrete precedent: OpenAI Moderation API, Perspective API, HateBERT, ToxDectRoberta, and RoBERTa models trained on each of seven different toxicity training sets plus their combination — [LMSYS ToxicChat](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-10-30-toxicchat/).
- HateBench benchmarks eight detectors including rule/threshold‑based and encoder systems (Perspective, Moderation, Detoxify original/unbiased, LFTW, TweetHate, BERT‑HateXplain) on the same generated corpus — [HateBench](https://hatebench.xinyueshen.me/).
- Jigsaw/Kaggle leaderboard results for a fixed test set remain the field's reference point for toxicity classifiers — [HF Jigsaw card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/jigsaw_unintended_bias); [Kaggle leaderboard](https://www.kaggle.com/c/jigsaw-unintended-bias-in-toxicity-classification/leaderboard).
- A fine‑tuned encoder can be a very strong domain baseline: on ToxicChat, domain‑trained RoBERTa reached F1 72.1 while off‑the‑shelf APIs reached 5.3–34.6 — [LMSYS ToxicChat](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-10-30-toxicchat/).
- For span‑like rules (e.g., PII), pattern/NER methods are the natural baseline; the 2026 evaluation concludes that detecting PII "means finding spans, which is a task for NER and pattern matching" — [Li 2026, §7](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Fairness protocol used in the 2026 System‑1 evaluation: byte‑identical `state` and `questions` to both systems; confidence defined uniformly as `max_k p_k` for choice and `max(p,1−p)` for yes/no because Laya's own API `confidence` field is entropy‑based and differs from `max_k p_k` by 0.299 on average (Jev: 0.010) — using it would "silently break any threshold"; accuracy intervals via Wilson; model comparisons via exact McNemar on discordant pairs with an asymptotic CI for the paired difference — [Li 2026, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- The same study explicitly separates a *reference LLM* from the paired comparison because its input prompt format necessarily differs, and reports a wording band of ~±5 pp for all absolute accuracies — [Li 2026, §3 and §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- LLM judges are biased: position, verbosity and self‑enhancement biases are documented (Zheng et al. 2023), and position effects can flip rankings when response order is swapped — [Zheng et al., arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685); [A Survey on LLM‑as‑a‑Judge, arXiv 2411.15594](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15594v3). Mitigations include balanced position calibration (judge both orders) and calibrating against a human‑labelled set — [LLM‑judge bias measurement guidance](https://befailproof.ai/learn/llm-judge-bias/); [Springer survey of LLM‑as‑a‑judge, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-026-11652-0).
- An LLM judge is not independent ground truth: in the 2026 study a blind judge agreed with dataset labels on 90.0% of cases both models got right but only 12.7% of cases both got wrong; changing the judge instruction from "strict" to "lenient" flipped 28.1% of re‑judged both‑wrong labels; the judge sided with Jev in 131/145 decided disagreements, but the authors refused to treat that as confirmation because judge and model may share preferences — [Li 2026, §8](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Vendor claims about adaptation baselines: Laya reports a fine‑tuned `laya-typed-decisions` checkpoint at 0.766 vs 0.362 for the base checkpoint on a 2,000‑decision typed benchmark, and a `Learner` path that reaches 84.7%→96.0% on held‑out rows after 0→120 confirmed answers; these are project README claims, not peer‑reviewed — [Laya README](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya).
- Constant/majority and near‑constant baselines are essential: Jev answered "cheap" on 99.5% of routing items, which equalled the balanced‑set constant baseline (50.0%) — [Li 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).

### Inferences
- **Fair comparison checklist (inference from the above):** (1) same item set and byte‑identical rule text; (2) identical candidate order, or vote over permutations and report flip rate; (3) thresholds/temperatures fitted only on a calibration split with at least several hundred positives for a 5% miss target; (4) report both per‑rule macro and prevalence‑weighted metrics; (5) include all inference costs (including fallback LLM calls and rule pre‑screening); (6) report paired differences and discordant counts, not just two accuracy columns; (7) treat an LLM judge as a *noisy, bias‑audited annotator*, never as gold.
- A *rule‑based* baseline should be rule‑specific: keyword/regex for explicit banned terms, NER/patterns for PII, and an encoder for semantic norms. A single regex baseline against a semantic rule set is a straw man; conversely, comparing a System‑1 model only against an LLM judge without a trivial/encoder baseline overstates its contribution.
- With modest compute, the strongest practical ladder is: majority prior → regex/TF‑IDF logistic → fine‑tuned MiniLM/RoBERTa per rule family → local System‑1 (Laya) zero‑shot → hosted LLM judge. That ordering mirrors the baselines used in ToxicChat/HateBench plus the paired System‑1 protocol — [LMSYS ToxicChat](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-10-30-toxicchat/); [HateBench](https://hatebench.xinyueshen.me/); [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).

### Gaps
- No source found reporting a standard TF‑IDF/logistic‑regression baseline specifically for community‑rule violation detection; this is offered as a suggestion, not established practice.
- No independent (non‑vendor) benchmark found for Laya's continuous‑learning/`Learner` path or for Jev's rule‑writing/fine‑tuning path on moderation data; the 2026 evaluation did not test Laya's fine‑tuning path — [Li 2026, §8](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- No head‑to‑head study found comparing regex, encoder, and System‑1 models on *rules that change over time*.

## Key Question 4: How do researchers report uncertainty: seeds, confidence intervals, paired tests, ablation studies, held-out rule sets?

### Takeaway
Established NLP practice is paired significance testing (McNemar/bootstrap) on the same items plus intervals for each score; for these deterministic System‑1 models, "seeds" matter less than *wording, option order, threshold split, hardware and model-version* draws, which should each be treated as an experimental replicate — and thresholds must be selected out of sample.

### Cited Findings
- The ACL "Hitchhiker's Guide to Testing Statistical Significance in NLP" recommends significance testing for the single comparison of a new system against a strong baseline on the same dataset, lists bootstrap, McNemar and sign tests, and notes McNemar is designed for *paired nominal observations* when both algorithms run on the same data — [Dror et al., ACL 2018](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128.pdf).
- That guide warns that NLP test items are often dependent (same article/author), violating i.i.d. assumptions, and that cross‑validation folds overlap; it proposes computing a p‑value per fold and applying replicability analysis with K_Bonferroni — [Dror et al., ACL 2018](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128.pdf).
- Practical application in 2026: Wilson intervals for accuracy, exact McNemar on discordant pairs, asymptotic CI for paired difference, no multiplicity correction (all reported differences had p<0.001; p>0.4 called a tie only where the CI was narrow) — [Li 2026, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Determinism/reproducibility checks reported in that study: re‑running 420 cases changed 0 Laya decisions and 1 Jev decision; on a second machine (Apple M4 CPU vs RTX 2080 Ti GPU) Laya reproduced 1,180/1,180 decisions with max |Δp|=0.006; one day later Jev changed 5/1,080 decisions (0.5%) — [Li 2026, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Replicate design for wording/order: option‑order reversal on 1,000 decisions; 4 instruction wordings × 2 case samples (600 cases each) per track; 500 random half‑splits for threshold selection; a separate 100‑case pilot whose labels were checked case by case — [Li 2026, §3–§6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Ablations/controls reported: B2 (random vs nearest‑neighbour distractors), G2 (transplanted vs channel‑native content), J2 (placeholder vs natural PII redaction), a two‑tier cascade ablation (local‑confident → Jev) and cost ablation (with vs without pre‑screen token cost) — [Li 2026, §5–§7](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1); [Laya README ablation table by rows/team](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya).
- Subgroup and confidence‑weighted reporting: Jigsaw's expanded test sets include subgroup annotations, and the competition metric was explicitly designed around subgroup false positives — [HF Jigsaw card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/jigsaw_unintended_bias); [Kaggle data](https://www.kaggle.com/c/jigsaw-unintended-bias-in-toxicity-classification/data). ToxicChat reports means with standard deviations in parentheses across training sources (e.g., F1 72.1 (1.2)) — [LMSYS ToxicChat](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2023-10-30-toxicchat/).
- Platform reporting uses 95% confidence windows from sample‑based estimation and explicitly declines to interpret sub‑window changes as significant — [Meta prevalence metric](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/improving/prevalence-metric/).

### Inferences
- For local moderation evaluation, treat these as the replicate axes: (a) rule‑set/calibration split (e.g., 500 random splits as in the System‑1 paper), (b) paraphrase of each rule description, (c) option/candidate order, (d) hardware and client location, (e) model version/pinned checkpoint, and (f) training seeds only for fine‑tuned baselines. Report medians and 90th/95th percentiles across replicates, not just a mean — inference from [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1) and [Dror et al. 2018](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128.pdf).
- For "held‑out rule sets", mirror the cross‑validation advice: split by *rule family/community*, report a p‑value per held‑out rule set, and apply a Bonferroni‑style replicability rule before claiming the System‑1 model beats the encoder baseline across communities.
- Always report discordant pairs (wins/losses) alongside the accuracy delta: the System‑1 paper's 455 vs 169 discordant wins makes a 13.6 pp gap interpretable in a way a bare percentage would not — [Li 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).

### Gaps
- No source found giving a minimum sample size for evaluating rare rule violations at a target FPR/FNR with a desired CI width; the only concrete number found is that several hundred positives were needed to keep a 5% FNR threshold stable out of sample — [Li 2026, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- No published study found that reports multi‑seed variance for Jev (hosted API, version string jev‑1.13.0 observed) beyond single‑day/next‑day reruns — [Li 2026, §3](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).

## Key Question 5: What are known pitfalls in calibration for softmax/fast-weight models and in benchmark leakage for rule-based systems?

### Takeaway
The principal pitfalls are (i) treating a model's confidence field as a probability, (ii) calibrating and thresholding on the same data, (iii) single global temperature for domain‑shifted rules, (iv) benchmark exposure and label noise, and (v) evaluation‑accounting errors — mislabelled metrics, omitted component costs, and confounded test constructions — which in one 2026 audit changed deployment recommendations more than model accuracy did.

### Cited Findings
- Modern neural networks are typically miscalibrated, motivating temperature scaling and ECE as the standard diagnostic — [Guo et al., ICML 2017, cited in Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1). ECE is a binning estimator of top‑1 confidence vs accuracy, so it is sensitive to bin count and hides local miscalibration in sparse bins — [ECE/calibration explainer](https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/calibration-machine-learning-confidence-accuracy-ece).
- A concrete System‑1 pitfall: Laya's API `confidence` field is an entropy‑based quantity on a different scale from `max_k p_k` (mean difference 0.299), and Laya checkpoints warn at load time that some temperatures are invalid and "confidence … uncalibrated" — [Li 2026, §3 and §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Single‑scalar temperature scaling cannot fix non‑uniform miscalibration (overconfident at high confidence, underconfident at mid confidence); it also preserves ranking, so it cannot fix a backwards‑ranked score (e.g., Laya's PII AUROC 0.41), and it requires logit access plus recalibration when model/prompt/domain changes — [temperature scaling limitations](https://mbrenndoerfer.com/writing/calibration-machine-learning-confidence-accuracy-ece); [production calibration discussion](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/16/llm-confidence-calibration-production); [Li 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Per‑domain recalibration helps: fitting Platt scaling on half the data reduced Laya's ECE on RAG relevance from 0.22 to 0.027; pooled ECE can be dominated by one degenerate track (Jev's 0.477 ECE on routing where it answered constantly) — [Li 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Conformal/abstention calibration needs enough rows: in Laya's own worked example, 10 calibration rows were too few for a precision‑0.95 review line, producing wide conformal sets and routing all 20 held‑out rows to review; the vendor states a few hundred rows are needed for an actionable route — [Laya README](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya).
- In‑sample thresholds are a recurring failure: a 5% FNR target becomes 10–17% at p95 on small positive samples when thresholds are fitted and evaluated on the same data; selected thresholds added at least one extra miss in 47% of held‑out splits — [Li 2026, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- The 2026 self‑audit found four errors that changed recommendations: omitted pre‑screen cost (23.9% → 4.3% saving), gate accuracy reported as end‑to‑end quality (58% vs 98%), in‑sample thresholds, and a "channel effect" that vanished when content native to each channel was used; two suspected confounds (random distractors, placeholder PII negatives) did *not* change conclusions — [Li 2026, §7, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Benchmark contamination is an established concern: worst case is training on a benchmark's test split and then evaluating on it — [Sainz et al., Findings of EMNLP 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.722.pdf); [contamination survey, arXiv 2406.04244](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04244v1). The System‑1 evaluation notes that all its sources are public while Jev's training data is unknown, so near‑perfect scores may reflect exposure; newly composed B2/G2/J2 controls preserved the conclusions — [Li 2026, §8](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Mitigations proposed in the literature: dynamically generated/adversarial datasets (DynaHate), LLM‑generated fresh test sets (HateBench), and variable perturbation of benchmark cases (VarBench, Findings of EMNLP 2024) — [DynaHate](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.132.pdf); [HateBench](https://hatebench.xinyueshen.me/); [VarBench](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.946/).
- Label noise is material even on curated sets: blind LLM re‑labelling agreed with dataset labels on 90.0% of items both systems got right, 75.7% of disagreements, and 12.7% of items both got wrong; one label-noise benchmark is explicitly catalogued by [Northcutt et al. 2021, as cited in Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- Rule‑specific leakage mechanism: if hidden moderation criteria are mined from historical moderation decisions and then used to predict those same decisions, predictive patterns may not represent genuine community norms — [Kim et al., arXiv 2509.02926](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02926v1).
- Base‑rate dependence is a pitfall in its own right: a gate's apparent savings collapse when the expensive path is rarely skippable, and the System‑1 study's 1.8% attack prevalence example shows how low prevalence changes the interpretation of false positives — [Li 2026, §6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1); [Laya README](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya).

### Inferences
- **Recommended anti‑leakage protocol (inference):** freeze evaluation rule sets before any model/prompt tuning; keep a private, newly authored "live" slice written after the system is built; split by community and by rule, not just by item; rotate rule wording and option order; report performance separately on public and private slices so exposure effects are visible. This follows the contamination and control‑experiment guidance above.
- **Recommended calibration protocol:** compute ECE and reliability diagrams *per rule family and per community* on a held‑out calibration split; fit only one temperature/Platt per deployed domain; verify score ordering (AUROC) before thresholding; use conformal sets or abstention rather than a raw confidence number for escalation; re‑calibrate after any rule, prompt, or model‑version change — grounded in [Guo et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1), [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1), and [Laya README](https://github.com/nandhakishorm/laya).
- **Audit the evaluation itself:** re‑derive every headline number from raw outputs, list every component cost, distinguish gate accuracy from end‑to‑end quality, use content native to each delivery channel in controls, and publish raw responses/case builders where possible — directly transferring the [Li 2026 self‑audit](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).

### Gaps
- No direct research found on calibration specifically for *weight‑updating fast‑weight / TTT* models (as opposed to non‑autoregressive System‑1 decision models) applied to moderation; whether online weight updates invalidate a previously fitted temperature is unverified.
- No reliable source found quantifying benchmark leakage for Jev specifically (training data not public) — [Li 2026, §8](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
- No peer‑reviewed replication of the 2026 System‑1 self‑audit numbers found; the paper is an arXiv preprint by a single author — [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02267v1).
